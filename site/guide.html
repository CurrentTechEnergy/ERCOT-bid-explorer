How offers work

Why thermal plants keep running when prices go negative. A walk through offer curves, minimum output (LSL), real-time dispatch (SCED), the day-ahead market (DAM) and on/off decisions, using one real day and real units from ERCOT's 60-day disclosures.

STEP 1

Two decisions, made on two clocks

Whether a unit runs at all and how much it produces are separate decisions, made by different parties at different times.

  • On or off (commitment) is decided by the unit's owner, through its QSE (the company that schedules and bids it), hours to days ahead. The QSE reports each hour's planned status in the Current Operating Plan (COP). ERCOT can add units through reliability unit commitment (RUC), but it does not turn units off to save money.
  • How much (dispatch) is decided by SCED, ERCOT's real-time market, every five minutes. It only moves units that are already on, and only within each unit's limits: between its minimum output (LSL) and its maximum (HSL), narrowed by how fast it can ramp. Inside that range SCED follows the unit's offer curve, running the cheapest offered MW first until supply meets demand (steps 2 and 3).

SCED never turns a unit on or off. So a negative price on its own does not shut anything down: it only pushes online units down toward their minimum output.

STEP 2

Anatomy of an offer curve

An energy offer curve is a list of price and MW points: "I will produce up to this many MW at or above this price." ERCOT draws straight lines between the points.

Two limits matter. HSL (high sustained limit) is the most the unit can produce right now. LSL (low sustained limit) is the least it can produce and stay online: its minimum output. Rio Nogales' LSL is about 475 MW out of 800 MW.

The shaded block from 0 MW to LSL is not a bid. While the unit is on, those MW run at any price; the only way to stop them is to turn the unit off (step 1). Whatever prices the curve shows below LSL never set dispatch or the market price.

Switch to "As ERCOT uses it" to see the curve SCED actually ran on. ERCOT fills the space from 0 MW up to the first offered point with a placeholder price (usually −$20), so in ERCOT's version the LSL block looks like MW offered at −$20. It is minimum output, not an offer to run at −$20.

STEP 3

What SCED can actually move

SCED reads the curve from LSL up. Drag the price: the dot is where SCED would put the unit at that price. It follows the curve above LSL and stops at LSL however low the price goes.

In each five-minute run SCED also respects ramp limits, so the real range is a little narrower than LSL to HSL: LDL and HDL (low and high dispatch limits). The chart below shows the whole day: from HE 10 to HE 17 the price was negative and Rio Nogales sat at its minimum output, because SCED had no lower point to send it to.

At negative prices the thermal MW on the grid are mostly minimum output that SCED cannot touch. The only thermal MW that respond to price are the ones offered above LSL.

STEP 4

The day-ahead market

The day-ahead market (DAM) is financial and voluntary. A QSE can offer a three-part offer: a start-up cost, a minimum-energy cost (for running at LSL) and an energy curve above it. If it clears, the unit gets an hourly energy award, a promise to deliver at the day-ahead price or buy back the difference in real time.

A DAM award is not a commitment instruction, and many units run without one. Rio Nogales had awards overnight and in the evening but none from HE 11 to HE 17, and it ran at minimum output through those hours anyway.

Some plants never use the day-ahead market at all. On every day loaded on this site, W.A. Parish, Limestone and Oak Grove submitted no DAM energy offer, so their on/off schedule is set entirely by their owners.

STEP 5

The stay-on decision

When a stretch of cheap hours is coming, the owner of an online unit chooses between two costs:

  • Stay on: run at LSL through the stretch, paying fuel for every MWh and being paid the (negative) price for it.
  • Cycle off: shut down and restart for the evening, paying a start cost, accepting a minimum time offline, and risking a failed or late start.

The figures here use the site's Stay-on calculation. For the example units they rest on generic costs (no three-part offer that day), so treat them as a rough guide. They often say cycling off would have been cheaper, and units stay on anyway. Common reasons are start risk and wear, minimum off times, gas already bought, ancillary service awards (J.K. Spruce 2 carried reserves through the stretch) and the cost of being wrong about the evening ramp.

STEP 6

Adding it up: what “From LSL” means on this site

Add up every online unit and the minimum output blocks form a large spike at the bottom of the offer stack. In ERCOT's version (step 2) the spike sits at −$20, which makes it look as though many gigawatts are bid at −$20 and are ready to move. They are not.

"From LSL" is how this site draws and counts offers instead. For each technology it takes the online units' total LSL and adds only the MW they offer above LSL at or below each price. The curve runs flat at the LSL total from the left edge, then rises with the real bids. The flat part answers "how much runs regardless of price", and the rise answers "how much moves with price". STEP 7

Reading the site with this in mind

Most charts answer one of two questions. Knowing which one helps avoid reading minimum output as a bid.

Commitment: who is on?

Decided by owners, hours or days ahead. Includes minimum output.

  • Floor-priced MW (≤ −$20 as used): almost all of it is LSL
  • Capacity by status, Running vs price, Starts per day
  • Thermal output in cheap hours, Shutdowns and two-shifting, Who turns off for cheap stretches
  • Inflexibility by technology: "≤ $0 as used" and "At floor"
  • Plant economics: what running cost and earned

Bidding: what do the MW above LSL ask?

Set by the offer curve, read from LSL up. This is where price moves come from.

  • Offer curves (the rise above the flat LSL part), Marginal supply, Marginal offer stack by price
  • Thermal offer prices, Natural gas and implied heat rate
  • Marginal supply by settled price, Changes in bidding approach
  • Wind and solar offer prices: wind and solar have no meaningful LSL, so their whole curve is a bid
