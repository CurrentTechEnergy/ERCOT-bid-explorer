    How the columns are built

    The list has every unit seen in ERCOT's disclosures since the site's data begins, so it counts more units than were online on any one day (batteries especially: many are new or run only some days). Group is the unit's timescale group in the latest forecast (how often it shuts down; thermal only). Capacity is the 95th percentile of each day's highest HSL (high sustained limit, the most it told ERCOT it could run). ERCOT type is the resource type in ERCOT's DAM files (SCGT90 and SCLE90 are gas turbines over and under 90 MW, DSL diesel and gas engines). Quick-start standby, Self-committed, Three-part offer, Day-ahead award and On outage are thermal only: the share of the unit's days in the last 90 days of DAM data with an hour in quick-start standby (OFFQS), with the unit self-committed (resource status ON or ONOS for at least half the day), with a three-part offer (a start-up or minimum-energy cost offered), with a day-ahead energy award, and on outage (status OUT all day). A unit can score on several, and a combined-cycle train counts a day when any configuration does. Each DAM behavior chip narrows the list: Yes keeps units on at or above the chosen share of days, No those below it (at "any day", Yes is at least one day and No is none). Name, county and zone come from ERCOT's Capacity, Demand and Reserves report, matched on the unit code; plant marks a unit matched only to its plant, which is how combined-cycle trains match, since the report lists each turbine. The search box also searches these names. Batteries renamed for RTC+B (names ending _ESR1) mostly match the report only by plant. Shut rate where shutting paid is combined cycle and gas steam only: of the stretches where shutting down would have paid at the day-ahead node price (the loss from staying on beat the restart cost and nothing held it on), the share the unit turned off for, averaged over 2019–21 and over 2024 on, counting only years with at least 8 such stretches; the bracket is the change in percentage points. Sorting on it lists the biggest drops first. Each year is on the unit's own page under Decision history, and every unit side by side on Will fossil turn off?, question 3.

    With operator info, by hour

    Hour by hour on the chosen day, how likely the site's operator model thought this unit was to be online, given only what its operator knew beforehand (its state, its day-ahead award and status, prices against its costs, its habits), against what it actually did. This is the model that sets the turn-off points in Decision history below; an hour where the dots and the line disagree is an hour the unit surprised the model. It needs the 60-day data, so it runs about two months behind.

    How it's built

    For combined cycle and gas steam units, the call made with what the operator knew going in: whether the unit was running at the end of the day before and how long it had been, its day-ahead award and resource status, the day-ahead price against its own costs and against gas cost, and its own habit. Each hour uses only what came before it. The line is the chance the unit is online and the bar is the call, on at 50% or more. Hours with a day-ahead award are shaded. The diamonds are the unit's Current Operating Plan as of 2 pm Central the day before. They are shown beside the call rather than used in it, since the plan is the operator writing down the decision itself. The top chart is what the unit did from the 60-day disclosure: output, and the minimum output of what was online. The wide shaded band is the day's worst stretch for staying on, the hours the decision is about. The reasons are for one hour: the unit's first actual switch that day, else the hour the call switches, else its cheapest hour (lowest day-ahead price at its node).

    Bidding

    Heat rate and fuel cost

    What it costs this unit to sit at minimum output, and what it asks the day-ahead market for the same thing. The cost is the floor a price must fall below before staying on loses money; an offer below it means the unit is paying to stay on rather than restart.

    How it's built

    Heat rate is EPA's hourly stack heat input (CAMPD) over the unit's telemetered net output to ERCOT in the same hour, steady hours only, 2019 on (combustion turbines: 2024 to 2025, matched to the EPA unit whose hourly output tracks theirs). "At minimum" is the hours ERCOT shows it running at minimum; full load is its top tenth of hours by output. Both are on net output, so the power the plant uses itself (station use: EPA gross less ERCOT net) is inside them; it is a bigger share at minimum, which is most of why the heat rate there is higher. A unit without enough matched hours, and combined-cycle cogeneration trains (whose net to the grid leaves out the host's load), take the technology median. The cost at minimum is that heat rate times the 7-day average of its own gas hub's price (each day's price is the trading day before's; Henry Hub fills a day the hub has none, and a week with any filled day is dashed), or for coal its plant's delivered EIA-923 price that month (the Texas average for plants that withhold theirs). The combustion turbine turn-on model reads a flat 11 MMBtu/MWh: per-unit values did worse there.

    Day-ahead offer ?

    Across days

    What the battery earned each day against a perfect battery of the same size that knew the day's real-time prices at its node in advance, how many full cycles it ran, and the average prices it charged and discharged at against the perfect battery's. Click a day to show it above.

    How it's built

    Energy earned is two-settlement: its day-ahead award at the day-ahead price at its node, plus output less the award at the real-time node price, hourly. Day-ahead AS earned is each day-ahead AS award at the day-ahead clearing price. The perfect battery has the same MW and usable state of charge range, its own round-trip efficiency (fitted from how its state of charge moves), the MW it actually held for AS taken out of its limits, the day's actual starting state of charge and at least its actual ending one, and at most one full cycle a day, scheduled against that day's hourly real-time node prices known in advance. Single days swing widely, so the 7-day line sums both over the week first. Full cycles is the energy it discharged over its usable range. Charged at and discharged at are MWh-weighted real-time node prices, with the perfect battery's dashed.

    With operator info

    The same days judged with what the operator knew going in (its state, day-ahead award and status, the price against its own cost and its gas cost, and its own habit): the chance it stays online through the stretch. A shutdown (hollow) above 50% or a stay (filled) below it is a day the operator model got wrong.

    Hedge formula?

    Whether this unit's offers reveal a hedge. A unit paid a fixed strike under a contract tends to offer each hour as a share of the day-ahead price minus a constant, and that constant is the strike; how low its floor offer sits says how deep into negative prices it will keep running. How offers work has the method.
