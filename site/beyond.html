Expected dispatch stack

Given who is committed today: the MW the thermal units expected online offer below each price in the selected hour, stacked by how often each unit turns off or by technology. The committed set is fixed for the day (the Overview's forecast: each unit's own day economics at its own node), so the flat block at the left is the minimum output that runs at any price, and only what rises above it moves with the hourly price. The vertical line is the day-ahead hub average for the hour; where the stack meets it is roughly the thermal output at that price. Every unit faces the same price here, so it is close to, not equal to, the Overview's expected output, which uses each node's own price. The floor uses the minimum output of the configuration each combined-cycle train was last seen in, so it can sit above the Overview's committed minimum, which uses the day-ahead limit. Units without an offer curve sit flat at their usual output; nuclear at its maximum.

Offer curves

MW offered at or below each price, averaged over the SCED runs in the hour. The line across the chart marks the cleared price for the hour; the shaded band is its range in that hour. With the 60-day report, "From LSL" draws each technology as the minimum output (LSL) of its online units plus the MW offered above that, so the curve runs flat at LSL and rises with the bids: minimum output runs however low the price goes, and only the MW above it can move by price. "As ERCOT prices it" shows the curves the way SCED sees them, with minimum output at −$20 (−$50 for some units) and the −$250 starts of some submitted curves. The Stacked view shades the LSL block from zero; hover for the split. More on what “From LSL” means.

Marginal generation by price

Who would move next if price landed at each level: each technology's share of the MW offered around that price, read off the day's offer curves (the slope of the bid stack). Battery charging bids are demand, so they sit below the line as a share of the supply offered at that price. MW at the −$250 floor (units with no offer curve, mostly nuclear and hydro) are left out, and so is each unit's minimum output (LSL), which SCED cannot move: on days processed before that was done it still shows as a spike at −$20 (−$50 for some units). Ticks along the top mark each hour's system lambda. The same view across every loaded day, by settled price, is below.

Across days, by settled price

Which resources move when the price moves, across every hour loaded, sorted by that hour's system lambda. Above zero: each technology's share of the supply MW whose offer price falls just around lambda, with each unit's minimum output (LSL) taken out in the 60-day data, since SCED cannot move a unit below it (the 2-day report has no unit limits, so minimum output stays in there). Below zero: battery charging bids that stop at that price, as a share of that supply (it can pass 100%; the chart stops at 100%). Through the day

MW offered at or below the threshold in each hour of the selected day, with the cleared price below. It follows the Offer curves chart's minimum output setting: "From LSL" counts each technology's online minimum output (LSL) at every threshold, since it runs however low the price goes, plus the MW offered above it at or below the threshold; "As ERCOT prices it" counts minimum output only from −$20 (−$50 for some units) up, where ERCOT prices it.

Who is at the margin

Marginal supply. Who is offering at the cleared price: MW each technology offers between the hour's lowest and highest cleared price (at least ±$1 around the average), from the hourly offer curves (it follows the Curve and Price selections), with each unit's minimum output (LSL) taken out, since SCED cannot move a unit below it. Supply shares add to 100% of supply. Batteries that stop charging as price rises are demand, so they are drawn on the other side of zero as a share of the supply in the window (it can pass 100% when little supply moves; the chart stops at 100%). It shows who stands at the margin, not which unit set each SCED price; with congestion, units can set price locally at prices away from system lambda.

Across days

Offers against fuel

Price at which 25%, 50% and 90% of a technology's submitted MW above minimum output (LSL) is offered, averaged over the day's hours. Minimum output is left out because SCED cannot dispatch below it; days processed before that was done use the whole curves, where the first quantiles sit inside the minimum-output block. A falling median with a steady lambda means units are bidding more of their capacity cheaply. Nuclear submits no curves.

Natural gas and implied heat rate

Daily gas prices: Houston Ship Channel and Waha (West Texas) from OilPriceAPI, Henry Hub spot from the EIA. Waha often trades well below the others and at times below $0, which pulls West Texas gas units' offers toward or below $0. Below that is an implied heat rate for combined cycles, combustion turbines and gas steam units. For each unit, the price of the first point on its submitted curve at or above its minimum output (LSL) that is priced above $0, averaged over its online SCED runs that day, is divided by the gas price that day. Starting at LSL leaves out the part of the curve SCED can never dispatch (many curves run from 0 MW at −$250 up to the unit's real price, and the part below LSL is just that line), and starting above $0 skips blocks offered at or below $0, so this is roughly the price a unit asks to move up from its minimum. Each day uses every unit's most recent value from the last 14 days, so the line follows the whole fleet rather than only the units that happened to run, and shows the median across units. Days with fewer than 10 units are left blank. The result is the heat rate (MMBtu per MWh) the offers imply if they were pure fuel cost. A steady implied heat rate with a moving offer price means the offers are tracking gas; a changing one means something else moved (margins, variable costs, the mix of units offering). The gas price used is Houston Ship Channel, the usual reference for ERCOT gas units (Henry Hub when Houston prices are not loaded). For West Texas units on Waha gas this overstates fuel cost, so their implied heat rate reads low.

Who is marginal at the node

Which units SCED dispatched to the interior of their own offer curve, run by run: base point strictly inside the ramp-limited range it had that run (LDL to HDL) and not on a vertical step, so the curve's price at the base point is the price SCED saw at the unit's node. A unit climbing at its ramp rate sits at HDL and does not count, and on days processed with ancillary service awards (format 5 unit files) the awards come off that range first: a unit carrying Reg-Up, RRS, ECRS or Non-Spin reaches its energy limit that much below HDL, and one carrying Reg-Down that much above LDL. Combustion turbines are scored without this, since netting their awards made their check against node prices worse. At lambda means that implied price is within $2 (or 5%) of that run's system lambda: the unit is setting the system price. Below lambda means the unit sits behind an export constraint, where its node is cheaper than the system (wind and solar offering at negative prices are the usual case). Above lambda means its node is dearer than the system. A unit marginal for a third of the hour's runs counts a third of a unit-hour. Days processed before the per-unit files carried per-run base points use the hourly mean instead, which lets a unit swinging between its limits read as marginal, and leave storage out; the last chart checks the implied price against ERCOT's published node price on the days that have one.

Read below lambda for storage and combustion turbines with caution. When their curve price is more than $5 from the node price, the node is dearer in nearly every case: the unit offered cheaper than its node and still was not dispatched up, so something the disclosure does not show held it back. A test on the 15 days with the most mismatches found that netting ancillary service awards brings coal and gas steam to about 90% within $5 but leaves storage and combustion turbines near 50%, and that ramp limits, ERCOT's SCED1 curve and, for most runs, state of charge do not explain the rest. About a fifth of the remaining gaps fall in 15-minute intervals where lambda moved by more than $5, which the 15-minute node price averages away.

Source: ERCOT EMIL products NP3-908-ER, NP3-965-ER, NP4-183-CD, NP6-322-CD and NP6-905-CD, gas prices from OilPriceAPI and the EIA. Times are hour ending, Central prevailing time. Curves are capped at each unit's high sustainable limit for the SCED run.
