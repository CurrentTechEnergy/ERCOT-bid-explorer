Offer curves

MW offered at or below each price in the hour, by technology, with the cleared price marked. What “From LSL” means.

How it's built

MW offered at or below each price, averaged over the SCED runs in the hour. The line across the chart marks the cleared price for the hour; the shaded band is its range in that hour. With the 60-day report, "From LSL" draws each technology as the minimum output (LSL) of its online units plus the MW offered above that, so the curve runs flat at LSL and rises with the bids: minimum output runs however low the price goes, and only the MW above it can move by price. "As ERCOT prices it" shows the curves the way SCED sees them, with minimum output at −$20 (−$50 for some units) and the −$250 starts of some submitted curves. The Stacked view shades the LSL block from zero; hover for the split. More on what “From LSL” means.

Who is at the margin

Who is offering at the cleared price in the hour, by technology, with battery charging that stops drawn below zero.

How it's built

Marginal supply. Who is offering at the cleared price: MW each technology offers between the hour's lowest and highest cleared price (at least ±$1 around the average), from the hourly offer curves (it follows the Curve and Price selections), with each unit's minimum output (LSL) taken out, since SCED cannot move a unit below it. Supply shares add to 100% of supply. Batteries that stop charging as price rises are demand, so they are drawn on the other side of zero as a share of the supply in the window (it can pass 100% when little supply moves; the chart stops at 100%). It shows who stands at the margin, not which unit set each SCED price; with congestion, units can set price locally at prices away from system lambda.

Marginal generation by price

Who moves next if the price lands at each level: each technology's share of the MW offered around that price. Battery charging that stops is drawn below the line.

How it's built

Who would move next if price landed at each level: each technology's share of the MW offered around that price, read off the day's offer curves (the slope of the bid stack). Battery charging bids are demand, so they sit below the line as a share of the supply offered at that price. MW at the −$250 floor (units with no offer curve, mostly nuclear and hydro) are left out, and so is each unit's minimum output (LSL), which SCED cannot move: on days processed before that was done it still shows as a spike at −$20 (−$50 for some units). Ticks along the top mark each hour's system lambda. The same view across every loaded day, by settled price, is in Marginal generation by settled price below.

Across days: who sets price, and against what fuel

Marginal generation by settled price

Who moves when the price moves, across every hour loaded, sorted by that hour's system lambda. The one-day version is Marginal generation by price, above.

How it's built

Which resources move when the price moves, across every hour loaded, sorted by that hour's system lambda. Above zero: each technology's share of the supply MW whose offer price falls just around lambda, with each unit's minimum output (LSL) taken out in the 60-day data, since SCED cannot move a unit below it (the 2-day report has no unit limits, so minimum output stays in there). Below zero: battery charging bids that stop at that price, as a share of that supply (it can pass 100%; the chart stops at 100%). Smoothed blends the 100 hours nearest each price, so each point rests on the same amount of data. Exact uses only the hours in each $1 band of lambda.

Who sets price, by technology

Which technologies are on the margin, weighted by how many MW they can still add at about their own price: at the system price, or setting their own node's price behind a constraint. Batteries are drawn on their own line.

How it's built

In every SCED run, a unit is on the margin when SCED dispatched it to the interior of its own offer curve: base point strictly inside the ramp-limited range it had that run (LDL to HDL) and not on a vertical step, so the curve's price at the base point is the price SCED saw at the unit's node. A unit climbing at its ramp rate sits at HDL and does not count, and on days processed with ancillary service awards the awards come off that range first (not for combustion turbines, whose check against node prices got worse with it). At system lambda means that implied price is within $2 (or 5%) of the run's system lambda: the unit is setting the system price. Below lambda means it sits behind an export constraint, setting a node price cheaper than the system. Above lambda means it sits behind an import constraint, where the node is dearer. Several units are on the margin in every run, so this is a share of the marginal units, not "the" marginal unit.

Room above base point is HSL minus base point on each marginal run: the MW a unit can still add at about its own price, which is what would pick up extra load. A unit marginal for a third of an hour's runs counts a third. Generators are shown as a share of the generators' room. Batteries are on their own line because their room runs from wherever they are, charging included, all the way up to HSL: stopping charging and then discharging, which is real flexibility but would swamp the shares, and part of a battery's HSL can be held for ancillary services or limited by its state of charge. Marginal units counts unit-hours instead, where batteries are many and small. The lambda filter uses each hour's average system lambda. Regions are the units' zones in ERCOT's Capacity, Demand and Reserves report (Bexar counted as South); newer units the report does not list have no zone. Days processed before the per-unit files carried per-run base points use the hourly mean instead and leave batteries out.

How well this checks against node prices

Read below lambda for storage and combustion turbines with caution. When their curve price is more than $5 from the node price, the node is dearer in nearly every case: the unit offered cheaper than its node and still was not dispatched up, so something the disclosure does not show held it back. A test on the 15 days with the most mismatches found that netting ancillary service awards brings coal and gas steam to about 90% within $5 but leaves storage and combustion turbines near 50%, and that ramp limits, ERCOT's SCED1 curve and, for most runs, state of charge do not explain the rest. About a fifth of the remaining gaps fall in 15-minute intervals where lambda moved by more than $5, which the 15-minute node price averages away.

Thermal offers and fuel prices

The prices at which 25%, 50% and 90% of each technology's MW above minimum output are offered, day by day, with the gas prices behind them and the heat rate gas units' offers imply.

How it's built

Price at which 25%, 50% and 90% of a technology's submitted MW above minimum output (LSL) is offered, averaged over the day's hours. Only units online in each SCED run count, so a quantile can move because of who is committed as well as how they bid: when few combustion turbines run, a handful of units weigh heavily. On Mar 12, 2026, for example, Waha gas was near −$7/MMBtu and Elk Station 1 and Ector County G1 and G2 offered most of their MW at about −$50, which took the turbines' 25% and 50% prices below $0. Minimum output is left out because SCED cannot dispatch below it; days processed before that was done use the whole curves, where the first quantiles sit inside the minimum-output block. A falling median with a steady lambda means units are bidding more of their capacity cheaply. Nuclear submits no curves, and there is no coal price series, so coal has offer prices but no implied heat rate.

Natural gas and implied heat rate

Gas prices at Houston Ship Channel, Waha and Henry Hub, and the heat rate gas units' offers imply against the gas at their own hub.

How it's built

Daily gas prices: Houston Ship Channel and Waha (West Texas) from OilPriceAPI, Henry Hub spot from the EIA. Waha often trades well below the others and at times below $0, which pulls West Texas gas units' offers toward or below $0. Below that is an implied heat rate for combined cycles, combustion turbines and gas steam units. For each unit, the price of the first point on its submitted curve at or above its minimum output (LSL) that is priced above $0, averaged over its online SCED runs that day, is divided by the price of gas at the unit's own hub on the last trading day before it, since gas trades the day before it flows. Starting at LSL leaves out the part of the curve SCED can never dispatch (many curves run from 0 MW at −$250 up to the unit's real price, and the part below LSL is just that line), and starting above $0 skips blocks offered at or below $0, so this is roughly the price a unit asks to move up from its minimum. Each day uses every unit's most recent value from the last 14 days, so the line follows the whole fleet rather than only the units that happened to run, and shows the median across units. Days with fewer than 10 units are left blank. The result is the heat rate (MMBtu per MWh) the offers imply if they were pure fuel cost. A steady implied heat rate with a moving offer price means the offers are tracking gas; a changing one means something else moved (margins, variable costs, the mix of units offering). Each unit's hub, Waha or Houston Ship Channel, comes from the Fleet economics assignment: the hub its own offers follow when they clearly follow one, otherwise its location (West and Panhandle nodes on Waha, everything else on Ship Channel). Units without an assignment use Ship Channel, and Henry Hub stands in when neither hub's prices are loaded. Days when a unit's hub trades at or below $0 give no heat rate for it, so it keeps its last value from the 14 days before.

Source: ERCOT EMIL products NP3-908-ER, NP3-965-ER, NP4-183-CD, NP6-322-CD and NP6-905-CD, gas prices from OilPriceAPI and the EIA. Times are hour ending, Central prevailing time. Curves are capped at each unit's high sustainable limit for the SCED run.
