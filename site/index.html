Will fossil turn off?

As wind, solar and batteries grow, do Texas coal and gas units turn off when power is cheap? Four questions, each answered by year from 2019: what turns off, why the rest stays on, whether that is changing, and what it costs in curtailed wind and solar.

1. What turns off, and what doesn't?

Fossil output has a floor it doesn't go below. Even in hours when wind, solar and nuclear leave very little load for fossil, coal and gas still run roughly 8 to 12 GW, most of it units sitting at their minimum output, which cannot come down without a shutdown. The floor has risen since 2021, not fallen.

2. Why does the rest stay on?

A day-ahead award or a self-commitment keeps a unit on; the price does not turn it off. Combined cycles that go into a cheap midday stretch with a day-ahead award almost never turn off during it, while most of those with neither do. Coal and gas steam stay on almost regardless.

3. Is behavior changing as renewables and batteries grow?

Yes, and in the wrong direction: combined cycle units turn off less often than they used to. Where a shutdown would have paid even after the restart cost, they now turn off for a smaller share of those windows than they did in 2019 to 2021, and keep more minimum output on through cheap hours as a result.

4. Where do fossil decisions slow decarbonization?

Most curtailment is transmission, not fossil. Most curtailed wind and solar is lost in hours when the system was not oversupplied, or at plants behind a transmission constraint. Only a small share is curtailment that fossil units at minimum output could have made room for by shutting.

Where a new load could use it

Most curtailment sits behind congestion, so a new load only uses it on the same side of the constraint. The best places for a flexible load to soak up otherwise-curtailed wind and solar are the southern Panhandle and the Rolling Plains, far ahead of Houston.
